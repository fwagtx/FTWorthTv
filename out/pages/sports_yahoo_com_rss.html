Players warming up on the pitch. Club officials — both from host New Mexico United and visiting Sacramento Republic FC — and workers going about their various responsibilities. Fans already filing into the seats. Others moving around the concourse.

In the hour leading up to the USL Championship’s Western Conference final, and two days after watching its Eastern counterpart in Charleston, South Carolina, the league president was eager to see how things would play out.

“I’m looking forward to this,” Alumbaugh said. “It’ll be fun.”

The nature of those words, “this” and “fun,” can be taken in a two-folded meaning.

About that match — a 2-1 win by Republic in extra time to set up a Championship final between the club and Louisville City FC in Sacramento on Saturday, which Republic won 4-3 on penalties after a 1-1 draw through another round of extra time.

And about where the USL as a whole is now and where it’s headed. Or hopes to.

The 40-year-old league received investment from BellTower Partners, a private investment firm, towards the end of 2025; a new collective bargaining agreement in place that runs through 2030; a first-time revenue-sharing deal that is set to begin in 2027; and, perhaps most importantly, or at least the item grabbing its headlines, is a division above the Championship — the USL’s current highest and one that sits at the second tier of the American soccer landscape — scheduled to launch in 2028.

And with it? A system of promotion and relegation.

“The whole premise has been, ‘How can we create more matches that mean something and get more people to come out?’” Alumbaugh said. “It’s a differentiator, right?”

‘A word we’ve used … is ambition’

Alumbaugh wants to clear up any misconception that might be out there.

The USL’s proposed top league — Premier — is not about competing with or rivaling Major League Soccer.

“You cannot take away from what MLS has done for the sport in our country,” Alumbaugh said. “They stabilized it. They had some rough times, too. … They’ve done a lot in the player development space. But it’s a big country, so it’s not a direct competitor to them because if you look at the things that they’re doing, they’re trying to become a top-five league in the world.

“We’re trying to create a promotion-relegation system that creates excitement, creates excitement, creates meaningful matches, creates more pressure and we think that’s a big part of what our country is missing.”

The outline of what the USL’s system will look like has been all been sketched out: No more than 20 clubs in Premier, no more than 20 in the Championship, and between 36 and 40 in League One in a three-tier pyramid. The goal is for it to be implemented in phases within a 10-year period, something the USL believes will be a calculated approach. Former Premier League executive Tony Scholes was brought on to lead the USL’s top flight.

Conversations started not long after Alumbaugh and Paul McDonough, the USL president and CEO, both arrived at the league office within months of each other in 2023.

Convincing USL club owners was another matter. No other American sports league does it.

Playing in a higher division comes with higher costs, but also comes with more income in the form of media deals, sponsorship rights and expansion fees from the revenue-sharing agreement, which will give the clubs access to those league earnings for the first time.

Competing in a lower flight means fewer costs, but less league-wide revenue.

It is, by the simplest means, a risk, but also a reward proposition. Winning matters.

“We’ll see how it develops,” United owner and CEO Peter Trevisani said of the USL’s revenue structure during an interview on The Glov Box Podcast in September. “I think it’s a really important step. If you’re asking what the bottom line’s going to be, it’s gonna de minimus. (But) you have to start somewhere. I think the league decided they need the owners and need to reward them for what we’ve done and on the flip side, we’re going to be providing the league with a lot of things that can help the league grow.

“I think what’s more important than the absolute dollar amount is the fact that we’re moving down this path and if it can continue to grow, it could be more substantial. While it might be more ceremonial today, it’s important. It shows alignment and brings everyone together.”

The Championship competed with 25 clubs in the 2026 season. League One, the second tier division in the current USL format and third tier in U.S. Soccer, had 17.

There are currently 20 to 24 applications for Premier, Alumbaugh said. That includes five current or former Championship clubs — Louisville, Pittsburgh Riverhounds SC, Sporting Jax, Atlético Dallas, which will start competing in the league in 2027, and North Carolina FC, which suspended operations after the 2025 season — that have expressed interest publicly.

But it also comes with some requirements.

U.S. Soccer rules dictate that for a league to qualify for Division I status, it must have a minimum of 12 teams in its first year and 14 by the third; clubs have to cover the Eastern, Central and Pacific time zones; at least three-quarters of the team must be based in metropolitan areas with populations of at least 1 million; all clubs must play a venue with a minimum capacity of 15,000; and an ownership group must have a combined net worth of $70 million with the person who has controlling interest (as little as 15%) having a net worth of $40 million.

Got all that?

“It’s ownership,” Alumbaugh said when asked what has the potential to make one application for Premier more legitimate than another. “It starts at the top, but it’s not just the ownership itself. It’s the alignment the ownership group has with the club’s business operations side, the sporting operation side. There has to be connectivity there.

“... And it needs to have the community support and the fan environment. So, if you wrap all those things together, a word we’ve used quite a bit is ambition.”

It is ambitious.

What, then, exactly will that format — that important system of promotion and relegation — look like once 2028 begins? That is still being sorted out.

“We’ve run, probably, 80 different models,” Alumbaugh said. “We know playoffs are an important part of the American soccer landscape and the American sports landscape, so you can’t completely lose those. But the regular season becomes more important because that’s how you truly get promoted.

“But then there probably will be some sort of playoff system for a second team or a third to get promoted. And then you’re also looking at relegation playoffs, where maybe if there’s a team in the Championship that finishes second to the bottom, they have to play against a League One team in a survival match. We think those are really intriguing.”

Think not so much the Premier League in England, which operates on a just a points-based system, but potentially — key word — more in the mold of a combination between the Football League Championship, England’s second division, and Germany’s top three flights — the Bundesliga, 2. Bundesliga and 3. Liga.

In the English Championship, the two clubs that finish the regular season atop the points table automatically earn promotion to the Premier League, while the top-six remaining teams compete in a playoff for the remaining spot.

In Germany, the 16th-place club in the 18-team Bundesliga competes in a two-legged relegation playoff against the third-place club in the second division. (The top two have already secured promotion and the bottom two relegated.) The same thing happens between 2. Bundesliga and the third flight.

“We’re studying it all,” Alumbaugh said. “We’ve got a really good team that’s working on that in our sporting committee and we presented some stuff in September in our board meetings to our owners. They had some questions and some thoughts, so we’re just going to keep working the model.”

And while that certainly provides the possibility of intrigue, it also leads to some notable questions.

‘It’s a big country’

The first?

How will the USL meet U.S. Soccer’s requirements, particularly with population and stadium demands before and when clubs are promoted?

The average Championship venue seats around 9,000 with 16 of the 25 clubs meeting the metro requirement. The average League One facility is 7,000 with seven of its 17 teams hitting the population threshold.

The stadium capacity obligation alone for clubs in both leagues would require a jump in seats — and financial commitments. Some teams, like United, which has played in the 13,500-capacity Isotopes Park since it first fielded players in 2019, are still attempting to find a permanent home.

“Look, it’s been amazing to play at Isotopes Park,” Trevisani said in September. “If the Isotopes hadn’t said yes and the city (of Albuquerque) hadn’t said yes, we never would have brought the team here. There was nowhere for us to play and really have a pro environment. But I think for so many reasons, it’s just not a long-term viable option.

“We need a place of our own and we can do more. We can bring a women’s team, but we can’t do that now. We can explore all those things. I’ll just say this, this park is about 25 years old. When they built this park, no one ever said, I don’t think, ‘You know, one day we’re going to have a pro soccer team playing here.’ But here we are and we need more facilities.”

McDonough, the USL president and CEO, has said in interviews he believes the 15,000-seat requirement doesn’t have to be a hard floor.

The USL’s position, Alumbaugh said, is having a stadium — whether 7,500 seats, 10,500, 12,000 or even 15,000 — consistently at 85% to 90% capacity is better than, say, a larger venue at 50%. It provides a better fan experience and atmosphere. Is having 7,000 spectators in an 8,000-seat stadium better than 10,000 in a 15,000-seat venue?

He pointed to Camden Yards, where the Baltimore Orioles play. The MLB club started removing approximately 2,000 seats from its upper left-field deck at the end of September for an open-space social area. He also used the Atlanta Hawks’ arena as an example when the NBA franchise removed around 550 seats in 2018 for a fan-friendly space.

“We’ve had great conversations with (U.S. Soccer),” Alumbaugh said. “They understand that in order for this sport to grow, it can’t just grow in the top-30 media markets. It’s got to grow in places like Boise (Idaho); Portland, Maine; Charleston, South Carolina.

“I was in (Charleston for the Eastern Conference final) and it was 6,000 standing room only (and) people lined up on the fence. It was wall-to-wall and a great environment. That’s important for our sport, too. Just as important as Chicago, New York, Dallas, Miami are. It’s a big country and there are a lot of gaps where there’s talent and there’s people who want to enjoy this on a night out and find the right places to go do it.”

In other words, the money that would or could be spent on stadium-capacity upgrades — although playing in newer venues and being the primary tenant is still the USL’s preference — would be allocated in different ways.

“Our big thing is we want capital to go into things that are going to improve the game, improve the players, improve the coaches and improve the people who work in the front office,” Alumbaugh said. “Putting money into facilities is still very important, but it has to be done in a strategic way, so training facilities and great places to watch a game. Rhode Island, one of our newer stadiums (with a 10,500 capacity), that’s a great place to watch an event. It has that feel.”

‘We’ve completely redone our approach’

The second question?

What will expansion look like, especially with several clubs between the Championship and League One either folding or reported to be shuttering operations?

In the Championship, North Carolina suspended business after the 2025 season, while Goal USA reported Thursday the USLPA and Brooklyn FC players held an emergency meeting amid concerns of the club ceasing its operations.

On the other side, the league is set to welcome expansion-club Atlético Dallas and AC Boise, which competed in League One last season in 2027. Those moves were announced in June and September, respectively. The Championship could see two more teams join by 2028 in Nevada-based Reno City FC and Ozark United FC out of Arkansas.

League One, meanwhile, has lost five clubs within the last calendar year.

Texas-based Texoma FC dropped to the semi-pro League Two after the 2025 season and South Georgia Tormenta FC went on hiatus ahead of the 2026 campaign. Corpus Christi FC, another Texas club, Florida-based Sarasota Paradise and New York-based Westchester SC have all either folded or are reportedly in the process. All due to financial difficulties.

League One is expected to replace those five over the next two years. Cascades FC (Eugene, Oregon), Fort Lauderdale United FC, Port St. Lucie SC and an unnamed club in Thousand Oaks, California, are slated for 2027 debuts. Rodeo SC (Celina, Texas) and a still-to-be-named club on Long Island, New York, are earmarked for the following season.

“We’ve completely redone our approach to expansion,” Alumbaugh said. “We understand, and we know, what it takes to be successful and to bring something special to your community, and there’s been times in the past — and we’ll hold our hand up — that we moved things along too fast.

“... Sometimes, teams have started too soon. Sometimes, they’ve started and not had the ideal venue. Sometimes, ownership groups come in and running a sports team is a lot different than running another business.”

That means, Alumbaugh said, a slower and more thorough vetting process of ownership groups and what their plans are — for the club, the city and the USL.

Long-term success has been valued over speed. Viability and sustaining power favored over a quick one-time showing of a 17-match home schedule in a previously untapped city or town.

“The Dallas team that’s joining next year, they’ve been in an onboarding process for 18 months to lead them up to their first game,” Alumbaugh said. “That wasn’t around five years ago and so those are some of the things we’ve invested in. We can do benchmarking.

“So right now, the Dallas team knows what Rhode Island did by this date in ticket sales, where they were with (merchandise), where they were with corporate partnerships, where they were with community engagement points. They can benchmark themselves against Boise and Portland. It allows them to say, ‘Are we ahead or are we behind?’ And that’s really helpful because data really moves a needle. You can say you think you’re good, but you’re 17% behind these clubs when they launched.”

If it sounds strict, that’s because it is.

All of it is for the end goal — or at least for the current one — getting a promotion-and-relegation system up and running by 2028. And after that? For it to be successful.

“We’ve said all along, ‘This doesn’t solve all the issues in pro soccer,’” Alumbaugh said. “There’s still a lot of things that have to come together. But we feel it is a good catalyst for moving the sport forward.”
